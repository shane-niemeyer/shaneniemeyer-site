The doctrine

Training is not something we do to our bodies a few hours a week.

It is the process of health and wellbeing itself, built into the fabric of daily life. Everything we publish, teach and coach flows from these principles.

Training is a way of being

Training is not a program we finish or a phase we pass through. It is a practice we build into the fabric of our lives and our daily process. The goal is not to complete training. The goal is to become someone who trains, for life.

Training is how we realize our human potential

Strength and power, muscular endurance, aerobic capacity, fundamental motor skills, every range and plane of motion, balance, body awareness, and the ability to get down to the ground and back up with ease. Each one we develop opens up more of life.

Aerobic fitness: as much as your life allows

The ideal is combined training, with the aerobic side carrying more of the weight by time and strength never optional. Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest predictors of how long and how well we live.

Meet people where they are

There is a path. It is everywhere and always, and it heads in the same direction for all of us. Where we stand on it is different for each person. Sometimes it is obscured by the weather of life, by circumstance, but like the sun, which is always shining above the clouds, it's there. Our work is to find where you are on it, and to travel it.

We start every person at their current level of capability and progress them rationally and methodically. Muscles adapt in weeks; tendons, ligaments, cartilage and bone adapt over months and years. Intelligent progression is what makes lifelong training possible.

Dynamic, periodized and built for recovery

Adaptation happens during recovery. Planned variation in volume and intensity, deliberate easier weeks, and room for life's disruptions keep progress moving for years instead of months. Rest is part of training.

Body and mind are one system

Our psychological states and our physical states shape each other. Training is one of the few truly transformative processes available in the human experience. It changes not just what we can do, but who we are.

See the target

We cannot hit a target we cannot see. We define, in as much detail as possible, how you want your body to look, feel and function, and then step into that picture. The clearer and more felt the vision, the more naturally the daily work follows.

No hacks. No shortcuts.

Some strategies simply work better than others, and we use the ones the evidence supports. The rest is the work itself, and the work is what transforms us.

Non-negotiables

What we never compromise on

  • Training is a lifelong practice, not a program.
  • Build as much aerobic fitness as life allows, always combined with strength.
  • Start where the person is, and progress at the pace of their slowest-adapting tissues.
  • Stay healthy first. Health keeps us training, and training transforms us.
  • Periodize, and build rest in by design.
  • Train the whole person: body and mind move together.
  • Make the target vivid, then show up consistently.

The process

We don't discover who we are. We decide who we are going to be, and we become it.

We are, right now, each moment, writing the story of our life. We are developing the main character in that story. If we are not, our story is being written for us.

Before we meet

Start with who you could become

When someone reaches out, I ask them a few things before we ever meet:

  • Their injury history.
  • How they feel, physically, and how they feel about life in general.
  • What their sleep is like.
  • How they characterize their nutritional habits.
  • What they want, what gets in the way, and what changes we need to make to create this life.

I have found it powerful and important to push someone's mind toward who they could become: how they want their body to look, feel and function. I ask them to describe an ideal future state and feel into that space. Then we begin anchoring around the thinking and behavioral habits that person would likely have.

Day one

A workout that is also an assessment

In the first session I walk someone through a workout, modifying movement patterns on the spot according to what I see: ranges of motion, quality of movement, discomfort. Often, you can see and hear how fit someone is aerobically. Without making anyone nervous, I can evaluate movement and identify postural distortions and compensatory patterns throughout the kinetic chain.

The plan

Completely unique to the individual

How much time do you have? What can you realistically commit to? What do you have access to? What are your preferences? From there, the minimums set by the ACSM are a floor, and probably still not enough.

3–5 hoursaerobic work per week, the target. 150 minutes is the floor.
2 daysof strength. The patterns look different for everyone.
~80%of total load aerobic, with intervals two days a week.

Every session

Structure, objectivity and polarity

Without structure and a plan, there is also, inherently, less achievement. Most people do what is convenient, or what they feel like on the day. They lack objectivity. Much of this work is about continually manipulating the acute variables: training type, exercise selection, intensity, modality and duration.

Many people lack polarity in their training. They never truly recover, and they never truly go hard.

So they never get the benefits of true aerobic states, and they are never able to train the top end of the bioenergetic continuum. When well-trained endurance athletes were randomly assigned to four approaches, polarized training produced the greatest gains in most key measures of endurance, including VO2peak. And people whose strength training was directly supervised progressed their loads faster and gained more strength than those who followed the identical program alone.

Stöggl & Sperlich, Frontiers in Physiology, 2014 · Mazzetti et al., Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2000

Every 3rd–5th week

Recovery is where fitness shows up

Sleep is essential. It is foundational. We build in rest weeks every third, fourth or fifth week, cutting volume and intensity by around 50%. This is when and how fitness manifests itself, and how we stay psychologically fresh and hungry.

It is a good time to catch up on life and tend to our inner life. Our minds can push us around in ways we are unaware of. I encourage us to take quiet moments, to look at the quality and contents of our inner world, and to be active participants in this process of recovery.

Over time

The north star is staying healthy

Progression depends on training age, history and fitness. Without structure and periodization, injury rates are high: among long-distance triathletes, studies report that anywhere from a third to nine in ten suffer overuse injuries. Most of these injuries trace back to training errors, and training errors are predictable and avoidable through intelligent, individualized design.

If we're healthy, we're training. If we're training, we're transforming.

Rhind et al., Journal of Human Kinetics, 2022 · van Gent et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2007

When we test

Prepared for the test, not surprised by it

Unlike many labs and testing centers, I believe clients should be conditioned for a test. There are risks, to connective tissue and psychologically, in testing someone too early or without preparation. We talk through the mask, the very high intensity, and what maximal actually feels like. It is a place we rarely go. Mild simulations on the treadmill make sure the joints and tissues are ready.

Always

Working with your physician

I work closely with several physicians and collaborate with a physician advisor all of the time. Fitness is the most powerful and far-reaching medicine we have for chronic disease and aging. No drug touches as many systems. Done right, it is a disease reducer, a life extender and a quality enhancer.

The evidence

The case for training as medicine.

Every claim we make carries a source and a label: what kind of study it comes from, and how strong it is. Here are the anchor findings. The full chapters are being written now.

~5×

Low fitness is a risk factor as serious as smoking

Among 122,007 adults given treadmill tests, the least fit had about five times the mortality of the fittest. The risk of low fitness was comparable to or greater than smoking, diabetes and coronary artery disease, and no upper limit of benefit was found.

CohortMandsager et al., JAMA Network Open, 2018

40%

Combined training beats either alone

Adults who did both aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity had 40% lower all-cause mortality than those who did neither.

Meta-analysisMomma et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2022

28%

Exercise changes cancer outcomes

In 889 colon cancer survivors, a structured three-year exercise program after chemotherapy lowered the risk of recurrence or death by 28%, and improved overall survival.

Randomized trialCourneya et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2025 (CHALLENGE)

58%

Diabetes can be prevented

In 3,234 people with prediabetes, a lifestyle program of at least 150 minutes of activity a week plus modest weight loss cut new cases of type 2 diabetes by 58%. The drug metformin cut them by 31%.

Randomized trialKnowler et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2002 (Diabetes Prevention Program)

= Rx

Exercise holds its own against drugs

Across 305 trials, exercise matched drugs on mortality in the secondary prevention of heart disease and in prediabetes, and outperformed them after stroke. Drugs, specifically diuretics, did better in heart failure.

Meta-analysisNaci & Ioannidis, BMJ, 2013

218

Training treats depression

Across 218 randomized trials, walking or jogging, strength training and yoga all produced moderate reductions in depression, with larger effects at higher intensity.

Meta-analysisNoetel et al., BMJ, 2024

The story Draft

If there is a point, it is how far one can come.

I understand, more deeply than most, how unwell a human being can be. There have been several inflection points in my life. These are the ones that made me who I am, and how I coach.

Jail

More unwell than anyone I had ever met

Addiction, and a bunch of insanity leading into prison. In jail, on my way to prison, I had a suicidal crisis. I tried to end my life, and I survived. That was the very bottom.

I was so toxic from drugs and alcohol and the way I had been living. Incoherent. Cognitively impaired. I was more unhealthy than anyone I had ever met.

Prison

The gift of prison

Then I had the gift of prison: time. Nearly two years of it. I had to start where I was, and where I was, was almost nowhere. I couldn't read, or even retain a sentence. I certainly couldn't do much in the way of push-ups or sit-ups.

Within weeks, and then months, I was able to read, eventually a book a day, taking notes on everything I read. Six months in, and then a year, I was in totally different shape than I was before.

What it taught me

Every system adapts to the demands we impose on it

That cognitive transformation is a testimony to it. Focus was a skill I could develop. Attention, and the use of my attention as a tool, was a skill I could develop. Memory was a skill I could develop. I treated all of it the same way: different stimuli to produce a desirable adaptation.

It helped my inner life too. If I looked at my thoughts, or wrote about them, I could examine them, edit them, and develop them into what I wanted them to be, to an extent.

If I sat with my emotional states, I could first identify them. Sometimes I could insert a gap between the stimulus and my response to it. A gap I never had.

That has carried into every domain of my life. Anything in our life affects everything in our life. That's why I work with the whole person. It has to be.

After

From a prison cell to Kona

I got out in my mid-20s and began training. My first race was the Pacific Crest half Ironman in Bend, Oregon, in 2005. Around twenty Ironmans and some sixty half Ironmans followed, with five qualifications for the Ironman World Championship in Kona and multiple 70.3 world championships.

2005first triathlon
5×Kona qualifier
9:16Ironman best
4:16half Ironman best

In the beginning I studied out of necessity. Now, every day, I explore human behavior, cognitive neuroscience, biology and cellular metabolism. For more than twenty years I have coached the widest range of people I can imagine.

Later

The work never stops

My divorce was one of the most important times in my life, because it forced me to do the work. To look at my childhood and early adulthood, and the intense experiences that shaped me. Some for the good. In many ways, I developed maladaptive behaviors.

It gave me the instinct to really look at my life, to dig in, and to become a better man and a better father. While it was perhaps the most painful thing I have ever experienced, in the end, after the darkest moments, somehow, it brought us closer together.

The most painful moments are usually the most transformative. It's hard to learn much, at least anything durable, when things are smooth and even.

Now

I still fail

I fail at things all of the time as a father. Failing looks like getting overwhelmed, or overreacting. When it happens, I take deep breaths and de-escalate. I tell my boys I can see that I'm overreacting right now. I apologize, and I try to repair.

This isn't like flipping a switch. That's not how it works. It's a process of staying oriented around our ideals and continually moving toward them, no matter how slow.

The only thing that matters is that we keep getting up every time we fall down.

Process · Science Draft

Never truly easy. Never truly hard.

Most people train in the middle. It feels productive. It keeps them stuck.

Shane Niemeyer · 6 min read

Most people do what is convenient, or what they feel like on the day. I understand why. But without structure and a plan, there is, inherently, less achievement. We lack objectivity about our own training.

The most common pattern I see is a lack of polarity. Easy days are too hard, and hard days are not hard enough. People are never really recovering, so they never get the benefits of true aerobic states. And they are never fresh enough to really go hard and train the top end of the bioenergetic continuum.

So they live in the middle. Every session is a moderate grind. It feels productive: you sweat, you breathe hard, you go home tired. But it is too hard to build the aerobic base, and not hard enough to build the top end. Weeks turn into months, and not much changes.

What polarity looks like

Polarity means making the easy days truly easy and the hard days truly hard. For most of the people I coach, it looks like this:

  • Around 80% of total training time is easy aerobic work. You can hold a conversation in full sentences.
  • Two days a week include intervals, hard enough that you can only get out a few words at a time.
  • Very little time in the grey zone in between.
  • Every third, fourth or fifth week, volume and intensity drop by around 50%, so the fitness can show up.

The easy volume builds the machinery: more mitochondria, more capillaries, a better ability to burn fat for fuel. The hard work sharpens it. We need both, and we can only do the hard work well if the easy work is actually easy.

What the research shows

When 48 well-trained endurance athletes were randomly assigned to four different approaches for nine weeks, the polarized group improved the most on most key measures of endurance, including an 11.7% rise in VO2peak. More threshold training and more high-volume training added nothing further.

In recreational runners, a polarized plan improved 10K times by 5.0%, against 3.6% for a plan heavy in moderate, between-threshold work. Across the whole group that difference wasn't statistically significant. Among the runners who actually followed their plan, polarized training came out clearly ahead.

Most of this research comes from endurance athletes, and the studies are small. It lines up with what I have seen across twenty years on the floor, with athletes and with people in their 70s and 80s alike.

Structure is the point

Much of what I do is continually manipulating the acute variables: training type, exercise selection, intensity, modality and duration. A plan gives us objectivity. It tells us when to go hard and when to back off, instead of leaving it to how we feel on the day.

If we're healthy, we're training. If we're training, we're transforming.

Module · The method Draft

Meet people where they are.

Locating the person, progressing the load, and periodizing for a lifetime of training.

Shane Niemeyer12 min read24 studies citedIn the book: Chapter 13, Training

There is a path. It is everywhere and always, and it heads in the same direction for all of us. Where we stand on it is different for each person. Sometimes it is obscured by the weather of life, by circumstance, but like the sun, which is always shining above the clouds, it's there. Our work is to find where you are on it, and to travel it.

The direction is universal: more strength, more aerobic capacity, more access to everything the body can do. What is not universal is how we apply the stimulus. The type of training, how much, how hard and how often have to fit the person in front of us, where they actually are, not where a program assumes they are.

This chapter makes one argument. Most training injuries are predictable. They happen when load outpaces the tissues that adapt most slowly. Locate the person accurately, progress them at the pace of their slowest tissues, and build recovery in by design, and they stay healthy. If we're healthy, we're training. If we're training, we're transforming.

I have watched this hold across more than twenty years and hundreds of people, from professional athletes to people in their late 80s living with dementia. Intelligent design is what makes lifelong training possible.

The method at a glance

We locate once, then keep relocating. Each block of training ends with a reassessment, and the next prescription starts from where the person is now.

Locating: finding where you are on the path

Everything starts with an honest location. Before we ever meet, I ask about injury history, how someone feels physically and about life in general, what their sleep is like, how they characterize their nutrition, what they want, and what gets in the way.

The question of what they want matters most. I push people to describe, in as much detail as possible, how they want their body to look, feel and function, and then to feel into that space. We cannot hit a target we cannot see.

The first session is a workout that is also an assessment. I modify movement on the spot according to what I see: range of motion, quality of movement, discomfort. You can often see and hear how fit someone is aerobically. Without making anyone nervous, I can identify postural distortions and compensations throughout the kinetic chain.

Then we measure. A few simple tests tell us more about a person's future than most of what happens in a doctor's office:

  • Grip strength. Among 139,691 adults in 17 countries, every 5 kg of lower grip strength meant 16% higher mortality, a stronger predictor of death than systolic blood pressure (Leong et al., 2015).
  • Getting down to the floor and back up. In 2,002 adults aged 51 to 80, those who needed the most support had 5 to 6 times the risk of death of those who needed the least. Each point gained on the 10-point test meant 21% lower mortality (Brito, Araújo et al., 2014).
  • Aerobic fitness. Each step up in fitness, measured in METs, meant about 14% lower mortality among more than 750,000 veterans (Kokkinos et al., 2022).

When we test aerobic fitness in the lab, we measure each person's own ventilatory thresholds instead of guessing from age-based formulas. That changes outcomes. In one trial, everyone whose training intensity was set from their own thresholds improved their VO2max, against 60% of those given standard heart-rate zones (Weatherwax et al., 2019). Pooled data from 1,544 people across 42 studies found the same: thresholds beat percentages (Meyler et al., 2025).

Locating is not a one-time event. People move along the path, so we keep finding them.

The slowest tissue sets the pace

Here is the fact most training programs ignore: the body's tissues do not adapt at the same speed.

Muscle adapts in weeks. It is constantly being broken down and rebuilt, and a few weeks of good training show up as real strength. Tendons, ligaments, cartilage and bone adapt over months and years.

The most striking evidence comes from an unlikely place. Nuclear testing in the 1950s and early 1960s spiked the carbon-14 in the atmosphere, and that spike was recorded in the tissues of everyone alive at the time. Decades later, researchers found the spike still sitting in the core of people's Achilles tendons, while their muscle showed no trace of it. Muscle had been renewed many times over. The tendon core had barely changed since they stopped growing (Heinemeier et al., 2013). The same team later found almost no collagen renewal in adult knee cartilage (Heinemeier et al., 2016).

Tendons do adapt. Across 27 studies, training made tendons stiffer and stronger, and the load had to be heavy to do it: tendons respond to high-magnitude loading, not light work (Bohm et al., 2015). But they adapt on their own clock, which is far slower than muscle's.

That gap is where injuries live. In adolescent volleyball players, muscle strength outpaced tendon adaptation, leaving their tendons under more stress than those of experienced adult athletes (Mersmann et al., 2014). The same thing happens to a 55-year-old who feels strong after six weeks and doubles the load. The muscle is ready. The tendon is not.

The cost of ignoring this is enormous. Among long-distance triathletes, studies report overuse injury rates anywhere from 37% to 91% (Rhind et al., 2022). In distance runners, 19% to 79% (van Gent et al., 2007). And most of these injuries trace back to training errors: an estimated 60% or more of overuse running injuries come from training-related variables (Nielsen et al., 2012).

Injury is most often the result of training errors, which are predictable and avoidable through intelligent and individualized design. So we progress at the pace of the slowest tissue, not the fastest.

Progression: enough stimulus, never a spike

Progression has two jobs that pull against each other. The stimulus has to be big enough to force adaptation. And it can never jump faster than the slowest tissue can follow.

Enough. Fitness responds to dose. When 464 sedentary women trained at the same intensity for about 72, 136 or 192 minutes a week, their fitness rose 4.2%, 6.0% and 8.2% over a control group (Church et al., 2007). People who seem not to respond usually haven't had enough. In one trial, 69% of people training once a week failed to improve. At four or five sessions a week, no one failed. And when the "non-responders" added two more sessions a week, every one of them responded (Montero & Lundby, 2017).

There is no age at which this stops. Nursing-home residents aged 87 to 96 gained an average of 174% in leg strength after eight weeks of heavy training (Fiatarone et al., 1990).

Never a spike. The danger is the sudden jump. Tracking 5,205 runners across about 588,000 runs, researchers found that a single run 10–30% longer than the longest in the previous 30 days raised injury risk 64%. A run more than twice as long more than doubled it (Frandsen et al., 2025). The risk is predictable, which means it can be designed out.

Strength that protects. The right strength work does more than build muscle. It protects tissue. Across 25 trials and 26,610 people, strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third (Lauersen et al., 2014). In novice runners, a hip-and-core program cut lower-limb injuries by 34% and serious overuse injuries by 52% (Leppänen et al., 2024).

So the plan steps up in deliberate increments, adds strength work that loads the tendons and joints heavily enough to adapt, and never asks the body for a sudden leap it hasn't earned.

Periodization: planned waves of work and recovery

Without structure and a plan, there is, inherently, less achievement. Most people do what is convenient, or what they feel like on the day. They lack objectivity. Periodization is the answer: training organized in planned waves, so that work and recovery each arrive on purpose.

In practice, it looks like this:

  • Build, then back off. Volume and intensity rise over two to four weeks. Then comes a recovery week, cutting both by around 50%. Most people rest every third or fourth week; athletes often go four or five.
  • Weight the aerobic side. The ACSM minimums are a floor, and probably still not enough: 150 minutes of aerobic work and two days of strength each week. I want people working toward 3 to 5 hours of aerobic training, with about 80% of the total load aerobic.
  • Make easy easy and hard hard. Most people lack polarity. They never truly recover, so they never get the benefits of true aerobic states, and they never truly go hard, so they never train the top end of the bioenergetic continuum. Most of the work should be genuinely easy, with intervals two days a week.
  • Keep changing the variables. Training type, exercise selection, intensity, modality and duration are all levers. We move them on purpose.

The research supports each piece. Across 18 studies, periodized strength training produced greater maximal strength than the same training without periodization, and programs that varied the load from day to day beat simple linear plans (Williams et al., 2017). In well-trained endurance athletes, polarized training produced the largest gains in VO2peak, 11.7%, while more threshold work or more volume added nothing (Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014). In type 2 diabetes, only the combination of aerobic and strength training significantly lowered blood sugar (Church et al., 2010).

The plan also bends to life. When work, travel or illness crowds training out, fitness holds on less than people think. In younger adults, strength and muscle held for up to 32 weeks on one hard session a week; older adults may need two. Endurance held for up to 15 weeks on as few as two sessions a week. The condition in every case: intensity stays high (Spiering et al., 2021). A well-designed plan has a minimum to fall back on, so a hard month never becomes a lost year.

Recovery and the inner life

Adaptation does not happen during the work. It happens during recovery. The recovery week is when and how fitness manifests itself, and how we stay psychologically fresh and hungry.

The research shows it plainly. Across 14 studies, endurance athletes who cut their training volume by 41–60% before a race, while keeping intensity and frequency, improved their race performance and their time to exhaustion (Wang et al., 2023). They did less, and they got better.

Sleep is essential. It is foundational. Regular training improves it in return: across 66 studies, exercise improved sleep quality and helped people fall asleep faster and sleep longer (Kredlow et al., 2015).

A recovery week is also a good time to catch up on life, and to tend to our inner life. I think it is very important to keep an eye on what we are doing and why we are doing it, and to live an examined life. Our minds can push us around in ways we are unaware of.

I encourage us to take quiet moments, to look at the quality and contents of our inner world, and to be active participants in this process of recovery. Our psychological states and our physical states shape each other. Recovery is where we tend to both.

A lifetime of training

I learned this first on myself. In prison, I had to start where I was, and where I was, was almost nowhere. I couldn't retain a sentence. I couldn't do much in the way of push-ups or sit-ups. Within weeks, and then months, that changed. A year in, I was in totally different shape than I had ever been. Every system of the body adapts to the demands we impose on it, if we impose them intelligently.

Then I spent two decades applying it to other people. Across more than twenty years and hundreds of clients, I can count fewer than 10 injuries caused by training. When we progress loads intelligently and reduce them regularly, keeping people healthy becomes predictable. And because they stay healthy, they keep training, and because they keep training, they keep transforming.

That is what makes the results possible:

  • A woman in her 80s came to me prone to falls, with very poor balance, afraid of the physical world. Within six months she was doing much better. Within nine, she was in a completely different space. After a year she was almost unrecognizable: roughly five to six times stronger, with far more aerobic capacity, able to get down to the ground and back up. There's a light on in her eyes now. Which, by the way, is a pretty common experience.
  • A high school runner was constantly injured and running well below her capability. Putting her in a healthier space made consistent training possible, and with consistency comes the development of fitness. She now runs for an Ivy League Division I program, with a lot of success.
  • A woman who started over 300 pounds, in the lowest fifth for aerobic fitness, has been injury-free for almost a year. Because she stayed healthy, we kept adding volume. She now runs, swims in open water and routinely rides for more than two hours. It is one of the most significant transformations I have ever seen.

The same care applies to testing. Unlike many labs, I prepare people before a maximal test. We talk through the mask, the intensity, what maximal actually feels like, and we run mild simulations so the joints and tissues are ready. Testing someone too early carries risks, to connective tissue and to confidence. The north star is always to remain healthy.

What this means for you

It doesn't matter where you have come from, or where you are. The only thing that matters is this moment, and what's next.

  1. Find out where you are. Get an honest picture of your capability: your strength, your aerobic fitness, how you move, how you get down to the floor and back up. Measured, not guessed.
  2. Decide where you are going. Describe, in as much detail as possible, how you want your body to look, feel and function. Feel into that space. We cannot hit a target we cannot see.
  3. Start below your limit and step up. Build in deliberate increments. Avoid the sudden jump, especially in a single session.
  4. Train strength, and weight the aerobic side. At least 150 minutes of aerobic work and two strength sessions a week as a floor, working toward 3 to 5 hours of aerobic training.
  5. Make easy easy and hard hard.
  6. Rest on purpose. Every third, fourth or fifth week, cut the load by about half. Sleep. Tend to your inner life.
  7. Reassess. Test again, and adjust the plan as you change.

Within a couple of weeks, we start to feel better. Within two months, we're a lot better than we were. Several months in, we can look like a completely different person.

This isn't like flipping a switch. It's a process of staying oriented around our ideals and continually moving toward them, no matter how slow. The only thing that matters is that we keep getting up every time we fall down.
