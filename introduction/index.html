Training is the process of health.

Why no intervention reaches further than the pursuit of fitness through training.

Shane Niemeyer10 min read19 sources citedIn the book: Introduction

Training is the process of health.

Training is not "working out." It is structured and directed toward outcomes. It is progressive in nature, and periodized to develop strength and fitness, which are among the strongest predictors of health and longevity.

The language I use with people in person, and the language I use when I'm texting them, is emotive. It's laden with charge. Sometimes I find myself sounding hyperbolic. Then I remember that I'm on very firm ground.

This book makes one argument, and the argument wins itself: there is no more far-reaching, pleiotropic, near magic intervention than the pursuit of fitness through training. Pleiotropic means one cause, many effects. Training is the most powerful and far-reaching medicine we have for chronic disease and aging. No drug touches as many systems.

A comprehensive, integrated view of training transcends just working out. It is truly a transformational process. It changes who we are as human beings and our experience of the world. And it has been demonstrated, again and again, to greatly reduce the risk of many different illnesses and to extend lifespan.

This introduction lays the ground for everything that follows: what health and wellbeing are, why movement is life, why no intervention reaches further than training, what training actually is, and how it changes who we are.

What we mean by health

When the World Health Organization was founded, it defined health as complete physical, mental and social wellbeing, "not merely the absence of disease or infirmity" (WHO Constitution, 1946). That is the definition this book works from. Health and wellbeing are one thing, seen from different sides.

Most of us were handed a smaller idea: health is what we have until a test says otherwise. But the absence of a diagnosis is not health. Health is capacity. It is the ability to do what we want to do in the world, to move through it without fear, to feel well and think clearly, and to show up fully for the people around us.

A healthy man wants a thousand things; a sick man wants only one. Nothing is more important than our health. Which is why I believe the greatest contribution we can make to the world is to improve ourselves, to give ourselves more capacity, so that we can contribute more to those around us and the world at large.

The north star is always to remain healthy. Everything in this book points there.

Movement is life

Our bodies evolved over millennia. All of our systems upregulate in the presence of movement. One of the most observable characteristics of life, of a living organism, is movement. Even now, movement is essential to survival. Certainly it's essential for our health and wellbeing and the optimal human experience.

We are subject to physical laws: gravity and resistance and time, all of the elements. We have to navigate through physical spaces and overcome a variety of different forces. A body that cannot meet those forces loses the world, one capability at a time.

Movement is essential to learning. Movement is essential to our psychological wellbeing. From subcellular organelles all the way up the histological chain to tissues and organs and ultimately who we are, movement optimizes all of those systems. In fact, there's not one system that's untouched by it.

Science is now mapping this, molecule by molecule. In 2024, an NIH-funded consortium trained rats on treadmills for up to eight weeks and analyzed 19 tissues. Every tissue they tested responded, with more than 35,000 molecules changing, including tissues no one associates with movement: the brain, the adrenal gland, the lung, fat (NIH, 2024; MoTrPAC Study Group, Nature, 2024). The human studies are under way.

Much of what the brain is doing is interpreting sense data from the body. Many different systems of the brain are involved in coordinating complex movement. Our psychological experience of the world tends to be a reflection of the physical state of our body, given that our consciousness, at least in part, is born of physical systems. And the brain itself responds to training: in older adults, a year of walking three days a week grew the hippocampus, the brain's memory center, by about 2%, while in a stretching group it shrank about 1.4% (Erickson et al., 2011).

No intervention reaches further

Start with what predicts how long we live. Among 122,007 adults given treadmill tests, the least fit had about five times the mortality of the fittest, a risk comparable to or greater than smoking, diabetes and coronary artery disease. There was no upper limit to the benefit (Mandsager et al., 2018). The largest synthesis to date, covering 20.9 million observations, found that high fitness meant 53% lower mortality from all causes (Lang et al., 2024).

Fitness is not fixed. It is a target. When the same men were tested twice, about five years apart, those who got fitter had death rates roughly 50% to 70% lower than those who didn't (Blair et al., 1995). When fitness moves, the risk moves with it.

And when training is prescribed like a treatment and tested like one, it changes the outcomes medicine cares about most:

  • Diabetes. In 3,234 adults with prediabetes, a lifestyle program built around at least 150 minutes of activity a week cut new cases of type 2 diabetes by 58%. Metformin cut them by 31% (Knowler et al., 2002).
  • Cancer. In 889 colon cancer survivors, a three-year structured exercise program cut recurrence or death by 28% and improved overall survival (Courneya et al., 2025).
  • Falls. Across 108 trials and 23,407 older adults, exercise cut the rate of falls by 23%, and programs combining balance, functional and strength work by about 34% (Sherrington et al., 2019).
  • Depression and anxiety. Across 1,039 trials and 128,119 people, physical activity was highly effective at reducing depression, anxiety and distress, reported as about one and a half times as effective as counselling or leading medications (Singh et al., 2023).
  • The heart and the brain. Across 305 trials, exercise matched drugs on mortality in coronary heart disease and prediabetes, and outperformed them after stroke (Naci & Ioannidis, 2013). In a 44-year study of Swedish women, the highly fit were far less likely to develop dementia, and when they did, it came 11 years later (Hörder et al., 2018).

One intervention. Every one of those outcomes, and more that later chapters take one at a time: the heart, metabolism and mitochondria, genes, the nervous system, immunity, bone and tendon and muscle. That is what pleiotropic means. No pill does this. Nothing else comes close.

What training is, and what it is not

None of those results came from working out. They came from training.

Working out is what most people do. They do what is convenient, or what they feel like on the day. They lack objectivity, and without structure and a plan there is, inherently, less achievement. Training is structured and directed toward outcomes. It is progressive in nature, and periodized to develop strength and fitness. It starts from where a person actually is, and it is aimed at where they want to be. We cannot hit a target we cannot see.

The difference is not semantic. In type 2 diabetes, across 47 trials and 8,538 people, structured exercise training lowered blood sugar meaningfully. Advice to be physically active, on its own, did not change it at all (Umpierre et al., 2011). People who seem not to respond to training usually haven't had the right dose. In one trial, 69% of people training once a week failed to improve their fitness. When the non-responders added two sessions a week, every one of them responded (Montero & Lundby, 2017).

And there is no age at which it stops working. Nursing-home residents aged 87 to 96 gained an average of 174% in leg strength after eight weeks of heavy training (Fiatarone et al., 1990).

Training asks something of us. It's both an exercise of self-denial, which carries a multitude of benefits, and with each session we complete there's an accomplishment that we couldn't have any other way. That accomplishment compounds.

If we're healthy, we're training. If we're training, we're transforming.

What the extremes teach us

We've learned so much about human potential through exercise. Human beings have exposed themselves to things no one thought were possible: swimming in incredibly cold temperatures for unbelievable amounts of time, running farther and faster in extreme environments than ever was possible. Tremendous feats of endurance.

I'll often wonder at these feats out loud to my clients, and remind them that while these extremities don't apply to them, they do point to what's possible in the human condition, given that we all possess the same systems. Broadly, we're constructed in the same way. We're made of the same things.

And while it's true that some have more available to them in terms of potential, for a variety of reasons, I have rarely met anyone who is operating close to their potential, either physically or as a human being.

I know what that gap feels like from the inside. I came to training in my mid-twenties from almost nowhere, and went on to qualify for the Ironman World Championship in Kona five times. I am not telling you that so you will want to race. I am telling you because the distance between where I started and where I arrived is the same distance most people are carrying, unused, right now. The work is to reclaim lost capability and develop latent potential.

It changes who we are

This work changes who we are, largely by changing what we are capable of.

Our psychological states and our physical states shape each other. As capability grows, so do confidence, resilience, mood and our sense of what is possible. The research sees it too. Across 218 trials, walking or jogging, strength training and yoga all reduced depression, and more so at higher intensity (Noetel et al., 2024). Among 266,939 people followed for more than seven years, the highly active were less likely to develop depression at all (Schuch et al., 2018). And regular training helps us fall asleep faster and sleep longer (Kredlow et al., 2015).

But the numbers only reach so far. A woman in her 80s came to me prone to falls, with very poor balance, afraid of the physical world. After a year she was roughly five to six times stronger and could get down to the ground and back up. There's a light on in her eyes now. No lab value captures that, and it is a pretty common experience.

I'm constantly musing about what exercise is and how it shapes us as human beings: how it facilitates the things we want to do in the world, how it helps construct a better experience than we would otherwise have. Where I keep landing is this. We don't discover who we are. We decide who we are going to be, and we become it. Training is one of the most direct ways I know to do that, because every session is a decision made in the body, and the body keeps the record.

The path ahead

There is a path, and it heads in the same direction for all of us: more strength, more aerobic capacity, more access to everything the body and the person can do. Where we stand on it is different for each of us. This book is built to help you find where you are, and to travel it. Fourteen chapters, in five parts.

  1. Why it matters. Chapter 1, Fitness is capability: fitness as the capacity to tolerate stress, and training, not working out. Chapter 2, How we know: what the evidence is, and how strong.
  2. The cell and the systems. Chapter 3, Two cities: the fit cell and the sedentary cell, side by side. Chapter 4, Metabolism rewired, and muscle as an endocrine organ. Chapter 5, The infrastructure: heart, lungs, vessels, connective tissue and bone. Chapter 6, Immunity. Chapter 7, The brain. Chapter 8, The mind.
  3. Exercise against disease. Chapter 9, Cancer. Chapter 10, The pharmacy: heart disease, type 2 diabetes, falls and fractures, frailty.
  4. Building it. Chapter 11, The two pillars: aerobic fitness and strength, and why together beats either. Chapter 12, Aging and the far end of the curve. Chapter 13, Training: how we meet people where they are, progress at the pace of the slowest tissue, periodize the work and recover by design. This is the Niemeyer Method, and everything else rests on it.
  5. The close. Chapter 14: a call to action.

Within a couple of weeks of training well, we start to feel better. Within two months, we're a lot better than we were. Several months in, we can look like a completely different person. This isn't like flipping a switch. It's a process of staying oriented around our ideals and continually moving toward them, no matter how slow.

Training is the process of health. Let's begin where it begins: with where you are.
